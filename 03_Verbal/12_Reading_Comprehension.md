## Passage 01 – Technology & Automation

**Difficulty:** Moderate  
**Questions:** 1–5

### Passage

In recent years, technology has significantly changed the way businesses operate. Companies increasingly rely on automation to improve efficiency, reduce operational costs and complete repetitive tasks faster. However, the growing use of automation has also raised concerns about employment opportunities.

While some people believe that automation will eliminate a large number of jobs, others argue that it will create new opportunities requiring different skill sets. Historically, technological advancements have not simply destroyed employment; they have transformed the nature of work. For instance, the introduction of computers reduced the demand for certain manual activities but increased the need for software developers, data analysts and IT professionals.

The real challenge, therefore, is not automation itself but the ability of workers to adapt to changing requirements. Employees who continuously upgrade their skills are more likely to remain relevant in the job market. Educational institutions and companies also have an important role in preparing individuals for these changes through practical training and skill development.

Ultimately, technological progress can benefit society, but its advantages depend on how effectively people and organisations respond to it.

## Passage 03 – The Paradox of Technological Progress


### Passage

Technological advancement is often regarded as an unquestionable indicator of societal progress. Innovations in communication, transportation and healthcare have undoubtedly improved the quality of human life. However, the assumption that every technological development necessarily benefits society deserves closer examination.

One significant consequence of technological progress is the growing dependence on automated systems. Although automation increases productivity and minimises human error in repetitive tasks, it can also weaken certain skills when individuals rely excessively on technology. For instance, navigation applications have made travelling more convenient, but constant dependence on them may reduce people's ability to interpret maps or remember routes independently.

Furthermore, technological benefits are not distributed equally. While developed economies often possess the infrastructure and resources necessary to adopt advanced technologies, less developed regions may struggle to access them. This disparity can widen existing economic inequalities rather than eliminate them.

Nevertheless, rejecting technological advancement is neither practical nor desirable. The real objective should be to ensure that innovation complements human capabilities instead of replacing independent thinking. Societies must therefore balance technological efficiency with education, accessibility and responsible usage.

## Passage 04 – Technology & Artificial Intelligence

### Passage

Artificial intelligence has increasingly become an integral part of modern decision-making systems. From recruitment processes to financial risk assessment, algorithms are being used to evaluate information and recommend decisions that were traditionally made by human experts. Supporters argue that these systems improve efficiency, reduce operational costs, and minimize human errors. However, the assumption that automated decisions are necessarily objective remains questionable.

Algorithms are developed using historical data, which often reflects existing social and institutional inequalities. When such data contains biased patterns, an algorithm may reproduce or even amplify those biases. For instance, a recruitment system trained on historical hiring records may unintentionally favour candidates from backgrounds that were previously overrepresented in an organisation. Although the system may appear mathematically consistent, its outcomes may still be socially unfair.

Another concern is the increasing difficulty of understanding how certain complex algorithms arrive at their conclusions. In some cases, even their developers cannot easily explain why a particular recommendation was generated. This lack of transparency becomes particularly problematic when decisions affect people's employment, financial opportunities, or access to essential services.

Nevertheless, rejecting artificial intelligence altogether would be an equally simplistic response. These technologies can provide valuable assistance when combined with appropriate human supervision, transparent evaluation methods, and regular audits. The real challenge, therefore, is not whether machines should make decisions, but how society can ensure that their decisions remain accountable, explainable, and fair.

## Passage 05 – Education, Employment & Critical Thinking


### Passage

The relationship between education and employability has become increasingly complicated in an economy shaped by rapid technological transformation. Traditionally, educational institutions were expected to provide students with specialised knowledge that would remain useful throughout their professional lives. However, the growing speed of technological change has challenged this assumption. Skills that are highly valued today may become less relevant within a few years, forcing individuals to continuously adapt.

In response, many institutions have begun emphasising practical training, industry-oriented courses, and technical certifications. While these initiatives can improve immediate employment prospects, they sometimes encourage a narrow understanding of education, in which learning is valued primarily for its measurable economic returns. Consequently, subjects that develop critical thinking, ethical reasoning, communication, and intellectual curiosity may receive less attention because their benefits are harder to quantify.

Yet, employers increasingly require individuals who can do more than perform predefined technical tasks. Employees must interpret unfamiliar situations, evaluate competing information, communicate effectively, and make decisions under uncertainty. These abilities cannot always be developed through repetitive technical exercises alone. They require a deeper understanding of concepts and the ability to apply knowledge in unfamiliar contexts.

This does not mean that practical skills and academic knowledge should be treated as opposing priorities. Rather, effective education should combine both. Technical competence may help an individual secure an initial opportunity, but adaptability and independent thinking often determine how successfully that individual progresses over time. Therefore, the purpose of education should not be limited to preparing students for their first employment opportunity; it should also prepare them to navigate professional environments that may not yet exist.

## 06. Passage

### The Hidden Cost of Convenience

Modern technology has made everyday life remarkably convenient. People can order food, transfer money, attend meetings and purchase essential goods without leaving their homes. However, this convenience often comes with costs that are not immediately visible. As individuals become increasingly dependent on digital platforms, they gradually surrender certain aspects of their independence, particularly their ability to make informed decisions without external influence.

Digital platforms frequently use algorithms to predict what users might want next. Although these systems save time, they also influence people's preferences by repeatedly presenting similar content. Over time, users may begin to confuse familiarity with genuine preference. What appears to be a personal choice may actually be the result of carefully designed recommendations.

Another concern is the gradual loss of patience. When services are available instantly, people may become less willing to wait, research alternatives or tolerate delays. This expectation of immediate satisfaction can affect not only consumer behaviour but also the way individuals approach learning and professional challenges, where meaningful results often require sustained effort.

Nevertheless, rejecting technology completely would be an unrealistic response. Digital tools have improved access to education, healthcare and communication. The real challenge is not to eliminate convenience but to use it consciously. Individuals must learn to question recommendations, explore different perspectives and recognise when technology is making decisions on their behalf. Ultimately, convenience becomes valuable only when it supports human judgment rather than replacing it.

## Passage 07: Edge Computing vs. Cloud Computing

For over a decade, enterprise software relied heavily on centralized cloud servers to store data and run heavy computations. While cloud computing lowered hardware costs and enabled global scalability, the rapid rise of Internet of Things (IoT) devices has exposed a major bottleneck: latency. When autonomous vehicles, industrial robots, or medical monitors send data to a distant cloud server and wait for instructions, even a delay of a few hundred milliseconds can cause system failures or safety hazards.

Edge computing addresses this challenge by processing data closer to where it is generated—directly on local devices or nearby edge servers. Instead of streaming every byte of raw sensor data to a central data center, edge systems filter and analyze critical information locally, sending only summaries or long-term analytics to the cloud. This hybrid approach not only reduces response times and bandwidth costs but also strengthens data privacy by keeping sensitive records on-site. However, securing thousands of distributed edge nodes remains harder than defending a single centralized cloud infrastructure.

## Passage 08: The Paradox of Remote Collaboration

When organizations transitioned to distributed work models, many leaders assumed that digital communication tools would seamlessly replicate office collaboration. In practice, teams discovered a subtle trade-off. While asynchronous messaging and video calls boosted individual focus and eliminated commuting fatigue, they also eroded spontaneous cross-team interactions—the brief hallway conversations where unexpected ideas and quick problem-solving often occurred.

Research into workplace communication shows that remote teams tend to become more siloed over time. Employees communicate frequently with their immediate project group, yet their connections with other departments weaken. To counter this, forward-thinking companies are shifting away from constant virtual meetings toward intentional documentation, structured brainstorming sessions, and occasional in-person retreats. Rather than treating remote work as a digital copy of the office, successful teams redesign workflows around clarity, autonomy, and deliberate connection.
